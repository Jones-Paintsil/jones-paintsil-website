About Me

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at Howard University and a Consultant in the Enterprise Analysis Unit at the World Bank Group, where I work with World Bank Enterprise Survey firm microdata. I am on the 2026-2027 economics job market.


My research is in applied microeconomics, development economics, and health economics. My dissertation consists of three empirical chapters. The first, my job market paper, studies how firms in developing economies choose backup power when the electricity grid is unreliable. I develop a model in which a generator can switch on instantly when the grid fails, whereas solar without storage is intermittent, which implies that outage duration should drive generator ownership and firm capability should drive solar adoption. Testing these predictions on 11,252 firms in 36 developing economies, I find that generator ownership rises with the length of outages, while solar adoption follows firm size, exporting, and digital presence rather than outage exposure. The second chapter builds a firm-level artificial intelligence readiness index for 44,221 firms across 76 economies and relates it to adoption and labour productivity. The third links drought in the first 24 months of life to the nutrition of 378,234 children in 34 Sub-Saharan African countries.


I use rigorous applied-microeconometric methods with large-scale survey and census microdata to produce policy-relevant evidence.

Job Market Paper

Outage Duration, Capital, and Firm Backup-Power Choice in Developing Countries
Abstract

Unreliable electricity forces firms in developing economies to secure their own power, a need historically met by diesel generators. On-site solar is now broadly cost-competitive with diesel self-generation over its lifetime, yet the generator still dominates. I develop a model of the firm's backup-power choice in which the two technologies differ in two respects. A generator is dispatchable and can be matched to an outage, and the model implies a threshold outage length beyond which running it pays, which makes outage duration rather than frequency the operative margin. Solar without storage is intermittent, and its large upfront cost makes adoption depend on firm capability and access to finance rather than on outage exposure. I test these predictions with firm-level data from the 2025 and 2026 World Bank Enterprise Surveys for 11,252 firms in 36 developing economies, with 17 high-income economies as a benchmark. I use linear probability and multinomial logit models with country and sector fixed effects, country-clustered standard errors, and the wild-cluster bootstrap. Omitted local conditions, measurement error in self-reported outages, and reverse causality could each bias these estimates. As a robustness check, I instrument outage exposure with the outages reported by other firms in the same locality, with and without local-development controls. The findings show that generator ownership rises with outage duration, while solar adoption responds weakly to outages and instead tracks firm size, exporting, and digital presence. The paper makes three contributions. It extends the literature on self-generation, which has treated backup power as fossil generation, to the choice between diesel and solar. It identifies outage duration, not frequency, as the margin that drives generator investment. It separates the dispatchability and capital channels, which explains why falling panel prices have not displaced the generator. Policies that lower the upfront cost of solar would raise clean self-generation but would not by themselves move firms off generators unless they also finance storage.

Private Sector Liberia Enterprise Surveys

Liberia's Private Sector in Focus: What the Data Reveal

May 12, 2026 Jones Arkoh Paintsil, Yew Chong Soh, Rose Mungai World Bank Data Blog

New World Bank Enterprise Survey data reveal troubling trends in Liberia's private sector, including declining productivity, reduced innovation, and persistent financial constraints. Real annual sales growth dropped from 27.3% in 2017 to 7.9% in 2025, while access to finance emerged as the leading constraint, rising to 39.8% of firms. The analysis identifies a negative cycle: without capital, firms cannot innovate, and without productivity gains, they cannot self-finance. It offers policy recommendations tied to Liberia's ARREST Agenda for Inclusive Development.
